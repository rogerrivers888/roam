Menu, order and stars — the table half of an evening

Google gives us the restaurant's website. Roam follows it, finds the menu, and reads it into dishes with prices and allergens — no photographing, no typing. At the table you tap who wants what; the order is grouped by person and shown to the waiter in big type. Afterwards you star only what stood out. This page is live: tap through the phones. Everything in it is the real menu at Circolo Popolare, fetched from menu.bigmammagroup.com on 4 September 2026, against the real household — Roger (vegetarian), Gina (carrot allergy) and Phoenix (9).

1 · How the menu got here, and what it costs

The question was whether the website Google returns can be turned into a menu, and whether that is expensive. It can, and it is not — but only three of the four steps are reliable, and the fourth is why the camera stays.

The honest numbers

  • Google: £0. The website comes back in the Place Details request Roam already makes.
  • Fetching: £0. It is the restaurant's own public page.
  • Reading a hard one: a menu PDF or photo through Claude Haiku is roughly 3–5p, once per restaurant per refresh. Fifty restaurants a year is about £2.
  • The catch: a plain fetch worked for none of the three menus here. Two are PDFs; the main one needs the page rendered. Budget for a rendering step, not for a download.

What we may keep

  • Item names are not protected; the descriptive prose is. A menu this household fetched is the same thing as a menu they photographed — fine to hold against their visit.
  • What we must not do yet is pool everyone's menus into something searchable. That is the copyright review still open in Technical Constraints (L9), and it gates menu search, not this.

What the ticks mean

  • A face is a person wanting that dish. Two faces on one dish is two of them.
  • Table is the Italian way to eat and the way the order should read: shared plates first, then per person.
  • The note under a dish (“no chilli”) travels to the order and to the staff screen.

The flags, and what they are not

  • Gina · Carrot? soffritto — ask A prompt, never a clearance. Carrot is not one of the fourteen allergens a menu must declare, so no fetched menu can ever say a ragù is safe.
  • Roger · Not vegetarian Diet and dislikes are grey and never stop you — they rank.
  • Ada · tree nuts (almond) A declared allergen from the fourteen would look like this and strike the dish out. Nothing on this menu triggers it for this family.

5 · The rating, as you described it

The rule

  • Stars mean good. One to five, on the dish, for the person who had it.
  • Nothing means fine. Not a gap in the data — an answer. Most plates end here and nobody has to touch them.
  • “Not great” is one tap, and only then does it ask why.
  • Words are optional everywhere, and the mic fills the same rows rather than a separate screen.

Where it lands

  • A star goes to the dish as well as the plate — a starred carbonara raises carbonara everywhere, which is how a rating in London changes a suggestion in Rome.
  • “Not great” lowers that one dish for that one person and nothing else.
  • Fine changes nothing, so leaving a dish alone is safe.

To sign off

  • Stars per person or per dish? Built here as per person — Gina can love the ragù while Phoenix does not. It doubles the rows when two of you share a plate.
  • How old is too old? A fetched menu shows its date; at what age do the prices get marked “as printed, may have changed”? Requirements leave it open. Suggest 30 days.
  • The “ask” chip. The research called for amber; the style guide has no amber and reserves red for the heart and for real allergen warnings. Built here as an ink outline with a warning icon. Amber instead?
  • Drinks. Included, and they rate like dishes. Say if a bottle of wine should be a table thing that nobody stars.
  • The set menu. Pranzetto prices by how many courses (£17 / £22 / £26), not by dish — the order total cannot be right without modelling that. Worth doing now or later?
