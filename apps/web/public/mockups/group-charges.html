A cost, from adding it to closing it

The last page showed a coach being edited and never added — and adding it is the harder half, because when you set it up you do not know what it costs. The owner's shape (4 Sep 2026): the organiser says what he has to get back (£360 for the coach), the fewest people it works with (12) and how many he is expecting (24) — and Roam does the rest, saying as he types that nobody will pay more than £30 and it will probably come out at £15. Then a deadline: “however many people are in, at that point we need to send out the bill for the coach because that needs to be booked.”

That is a cost with a life, not a number in a field: open while people join and nobody pays, closed on a date when the headcount fixes the price and the bill goes out, and cancelled if it never reached twelve. This page draws it end to end — adding it from nothing, the two ways of typing the same prices, the moment it closes, and what everybody reads at each stage.

Two costs of two different shapes, and one number for what the whole thing has cost him so far. Editing is the pencil; this is where the last page started.

What the third screen is really asking

  • Nobody knows what a coach costs per head when they set a group up — they know the quote, the fewest people it works with, and roughly how many are coming.
  • So it asks for exactly those three and does the division, showing the answer beside each number as it is typed. It never asks “how much do you want to charge?”, which is the question with no answer in September.

The three states of a cost

  • Open — people joining, price a range, nobody pays.
  • Closed — headcount fixed, price fixed, bill out, and the money is due.
  • Cancelled — it never reached twelve, so nothing was booked and nothing was taken.

2 · Three numbers, and the story they tell

The organiser types what he has to get back, the fewest people it works with and how many he expects. Everything else is arithmetic, and it is shown as he types rather than asked for: the number beside the minimum is the most anybody will ever pay, and the number beside the expected is what it will probably come out at.

There is no third figure to type and no cap to keep in step with anything. The ceiling is the minimum: the only way a share could go above £30 is if fewer than twelve came, and then it does not happen at all. That is the whole promise, and it is one sentence — “it will not cost you more than £30, and probably about £15”.

And no floor either

  • If thirty come it is £12 each and everybody pays £12. The total is what it is; extra people make it cheaper for everyone and there is never a surplus to argue about.
  • That removes a question the last draft had to ask — who keeps the difference — because there is no difference to keep.

Rounding, honestly

  • Shares are rounded up to the penny so the £360 is always covered. At 17 people that is £21.18 each — six pence over, which stays with the organiser.
  • Never down, because then his own app has left him short.

The one it will not let him do

  • Type a total the numbers cannot carry. If the quote is £420 and the minimum is 12, the most anybody pays is £35 — the screen says so as he types rather than letting him promise £30 and find out in October.

3 · The deadline is the whole design

“However many people are in, at that point we need to send out the bill for the coach because that needs to be booked.” Everything about a variable price follows from that one sentence: nobody pays while the price can still move, and the day it stops moving is the day the money is asked for and the day it stops being refundable — because that is the day the organiser's own money leaves.

The failure is a screen with three ways out, not a dead end. Breaking the promised cap is possible and is treated as what it is: a new offer.

The rules that fall out of the deadline

  • Nobody is billed before it closes. With a moving price there is nothing true to bill.
  • Refundable until it closes is the default, and it needs no refunds at all — nothing has been taken.
  • Not refundable after, because that is the day the organiser's money goes. Dropping out afterwards still owes.
  • Late joiners pay the settled price, up to the capacity that was booked. The surplus is the organiser's — he is the one carrying the risk.
  • Raising the ceiling is a new offer — changing the total or the minimum after people are in means asking them again.

Two things it must never do

  • Take money while the price can still change.
  • Charge somebody more than the ceiling they were told, without asking them.

5 · What this is, underneath

Everything above is one row in a table with eight columns and three states. Written down so the build is a day's work rather than a discussion.

A cost hasand it means
A name and a sentenceThe organiser's own words, shown at the point of paying
A way of pricingfixed — the same each · varies — a total divided by heads · (later: bands)
Per head or per partyWhether Leo is a share
Everyone, or opt-inHalf the club drives; only the yeses are divided by and billed
A total, a minimum and an expected numberThe three the organiser types. The ceiling (total ÷ minimum) and the likely price (total ÷ expected) are worked out, never entered
A minimum, and a capacityBelow the minimum nothing runs; above the capacity nobody else can join
A closing dateWhen the headcount and the price stop moving and the bill goes out
A stateopen → closed (with a settled price and a due date) → settled, or cancelled

What that costs to build

  • The three screens for adding one and the cost list: half a day on top of what exists.
  • The arithmetic and the states: the division, the ceiling that falls out of the minimum, the closing job and the bill. A day.
  • Nothing new is needed to take money — the bill is a line that says what is owed and by when, and the organiser ticks it off, exactly as now.
  • The closing job runs on the same loop that already writes the reminders.

What it gives the reminders

  • “Four more and the coach runs” instead of “you have something outstanding”. The reminders already exist; this gives them something worth saying.

6 · The calls to make

Does anybody pay before it closes?No. While the price can move there is nothing true to charge.It also means "refundable until it closes" needs no refunds at all — nothing has been taken. That is the single biggest simplification available.
Can people join after it closes?Yes, up to the capacity that was booked, at the settled price. The organiser sets the capacity when he adds the cost, and can turn it off.The owner's open question. It is the coach's own seat count, and the organiser is the only one who knows it — so it is a field, not a rule.
Can the price go above the ceiling?Only by asking everybody again.“No more than £30” is the promise that got the yes. Quietly exceeding it is the one thing that would make an organiser look bad in front of their own club.
What is called off when the minimum is missed?The cost — the coach does not run and the weekend still does. But a minibus to a match is sometimes the whole thing, and the organiser is the only one who knows which this is.
Does Roam close it automatically?It asks the first time and does it by itself after that, with the organiser able to move the date or call it off from the same screen.A deadline nobody enforces is a suggestion; a deadline enforced without warning is a shock.

The one-paragraph version: a cost is added in three screens — what it is and who pays a share, how the price works, and when it closes. A varying price is three numbers: what you have to get back, the fewest people it works with and how many you expect. Beside the first of those Roam shows the ceiling (£360 ÷ 12 = £30, the most anybody will ever pay) and beside the second the likely price (£360 ÷ 24 = £15), so the story tells itself as it is typed. Nobody pays while it can still move: everyone sees a range with a ceiling and the number it needs. On the closing day the headcount fixes the price, the bill goes out with its own arithmetic on it, and that is the day it stops being refundable — because that is the day the organiser's money leaves. If it never reached the minimum it is called off that morning and nothing was ever taken.
