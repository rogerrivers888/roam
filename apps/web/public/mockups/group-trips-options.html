Group trips — how the group is run, three ways

Twenty-four people going to Cardiff for a weekend. One of them is carrying it: he has picked the hotel, hired a coach, and now spends six weeks asking people whether they have booked, whether they have paid him, and whether their son is coming after all. That chasing is the work, and it happens in a group chat with no record of who was asked. Roam already holds the trip — the dates, the hotel, the two things they are doing — so the group can hang off it. This page takes Roam — Group Trips v1.0 and asks the three questions it leaves open: what the organiser's screen is, how someone joins from a link, and what happens when money is involved. Each has three answers drawn out, one recommended.

Throughout: Cardiff · rugby tour, Fri 13 – Sun 15 November 2026. Roger is the organiser. Twenty-four expected. Everyone books their own room at the Parkgate; everyone books the stadium tour; everyone pays Roger £45 for the coach and the match tickets he has already bought. That is exactly the shape v1 describes: two supplier bookings and one flat amount he is out of pocket for.

1 · What a group is, in Roam's own model

Roam's unit of account is the household: members, their allergens, their tastes, everything they have rated. A group is not a bigger household, and it must not become one — the organiser is being handed a list of names and a payment status, not twenty-four people's dietary opinions. Two layers, drawn once so the rest of the page can lean on it.

The item points at the trip's place; the name is read from the owned record at display.

Three consequences

  • A participant is a party, not a person. Priya joins and brings Leo, 15. One row, two heads. Headcount for the coach is heads; the checklist is per party.
  • A minor is not a payer. The household model already knows who is a minor. In a group the parent's row carries the child, so nobody asks a fifteen-year-old for a card.
  • Possession of the link is not identity. Anyone the link reaches can open it, so the name and contact typed on joining is all we know — and the organiser can see it is all we know.

What the trip already gives us

  • The base (the Parkgate, two nights) is the accommodation item.
  • A stop (the stadium tour, Saturday morning) is an activity item.
  • Attendees, dates, and the day plan are unchanged — the group hangs off them.

3 · Joining from a link — three ways

Priya gets a link in a WhatsApp group at 21:40. She has never heard of Roam. Whatever she does next, the organiser needs a name he recognises and a way to reach her, and Roam needs her to still be here in two minutes. The doc is firm on one thing: the link proves nothing about who is holding it, so identity is only ever what she types.

Included because it is what most products do, and because the doc rules it out in one line: joining cannot depend on having an account.

Why 2 wins

  • The doc's acceptance criterion is that a person who supplies name and contact joins and appears immediately in the organiser view. It does not say that has to be the first screen — and asking at the first action gets a truer name, because by then she knows who is going to read it.
  • It is the same screen she will use for the next six weeks. Joining is not a separate flow; it is the first tap on her checklist.
  • 1 is the fallback, not a rejection: when the organiser added her by name beforehand, the sheet opens pre-filled with “Are you Priya Shah?” and joining is one tap.

The four link edges, whichever wins

  • Opened twice — the same device resumes as the same participant. Never a second row.
  • Regenerated — the old link says “Roger has issued a new link, ask him for it”. Everyone already joined is untouched.
  • Group closed — “this group is not taking any more people”, with the organiser's name and no way in.
  • Matched to a name — the organiser had added “Priya S.”; her join lands on that row rather than making a new one, and he is told they were matched.

What she is not asked

  • An account, a password, or a card, to see the trip.
  • Her allergens — unless she chooses to add them, and then they travel to the restaurant booking as a requirement, not onto Roger's roster of who has paid.
  • Anything about her tastes. The group is a checklist, not a profile.

The un-keyed prototype

  • Nothing on this page requires a Stripe key. Adding one, and setting any spend or payout cap, is yours to do — through Doppler, never in the repo, never as a Railway variable.
  • Which means the first build can go all the way to the payment sheet and stop: the button says Mark as paid and the organiser ticks it. Every other screen is unchanged when the key arrives.

5 · The part a spreadsheet cannot do

Everything up to here — names, ticks, chasing — a spreadsheet does badly but does. These two do not exist in a spreadsheet, and they are the reason a group would rather run on Roam: the trip already knows the plan, and Roam already knows how to read a place. Both are v1-cheap, because neither needs money or a partner.

Twenty-six covers, three allergies, and a menu Roam can already read. This is the group feature nobody asked for and everybody uses.

Both are cheap

  • No new provider call. The hotel is the trip's base; the menu is the one Roam already fetches from the restaurant's own site when a place is opened.

And one warning

  • A count of allergies is a health fact about a named person. It is theirs, not the organiser's: they choose to add it, they can take it away, and it is not part of what an organiser can export.

6 · The rules this has to obey

Group trips reach into every part of Roam that has a rule attached to it. None of these are new decisions; they are existing constraints, and what each one means the moment there are twenty-four people instead of four.

7 · The calls to make

The doc leaves twenty-one open questions across seven epics. These eight are the ones that change what gets built; the rest can be answered while it is being built. Each has what I would do, so a nod is enough.

QuestionWhat I would doWhy, and what it blocks
Does v1 collect money at all?
Epic 4		Ship v1 without it. The fee item exists, shows the amount and the organiser's words, and the organiser ticks people off by hand. The pay button appears when Stripe Connect and verification are in place.Everything else in the doc works without a payment provider, a platform account, a verification flow, a refund policy or a published privacy notice. Waiting for those five things to ship a group that can be created and chased is the wrong order. Blocks nothing if answered “no”; blocks six weeks if answered “yes”.
What must a participant supply?
Epic 3		A name and one contact, their choice of mobile or email. Mobile is what an organiser actually has for people.Decides the reminder channel, the join screen, and whether an organiser adding someone by name can be matched to them. Blocks the join screen.
How is a minor handled?
Epic 3, business constraints		A minor is never a participant of their own. A parent's row carries them as a head, pays for them, and books for them. A group that is all minors — a school trip — is a different product and is out of v1.Rugby tours and school trips are the same shape until money and consent appear, when they are not. Blocks the participant model.
Are bookings confirmed back to us?
Epic 5		Assume not. Design self-declaration as the main path and treat any confirmation from a partner as a bonus that upgrades a row from “his word” to “confirmed”.Without a partner agreement there is no attribution at all, and the doc's organiser view is built on it. Designing for self-declaration first means the screens do not change when a partner arrives. Blocks the whole of Epic 5 if answered the other way.
Reminders — by what?
Epic 6		Whatever they gave, one channel each, and a hard limit of one reminder per person per item per 48 hours.Outbound contact on someone else's behalf is the part with a real cost and a real complaint attached. Blocks the reminder flow and the privacy notice.
Six or thirty?
Appendix A2		Build for thirty; check every screen at six. Option B reads the same at both.Decides whether the roster is the screen or a section of it.
Can the organiser hand it over?
Epic 1		Not in v1. State it plainly at creation.Transferring a group with settled money in it is a much bigger question than it looks.
Day trips as well as weekends?
Appendix A3		Yes, free. An outing is a trip with one day in Roam's model, so a group hangs off it unchanged — and a minibus to a match is the same three items.Costs nothing to allow and doubles what the feature is for.

What I would build first, in order

  • 1 · A group on a trip. Create it, name it, say how many are expected. The trip becomes a group trip in the list.
  • 2 · The checklist. Mark the base and any stops as wanted; add the fee as an amount and a description, with no way to pay it yet.
  • 3 · The link and joining. Name, one contact, who they bring. Resume, regenerate, close, and match-to-a-name.
  • 4 · The two views. Option B for the organiser; the three-item checklist for the participant. Bookings are self-declared.
  • 5 · Chasing. One reminder per item, recorded against the person with a date.
  • 6 · Then money, when the Stripe side exists — and nothing above changes.

What this page does not answer

  • What the organiser's web view looks like at 1000px. Sketched in the table, not drawn. It is the next mock-up if B is the direction.
  • Splitting one booking between people — one room, four ways. That is v2 and it needs Roam to be taking the money.
  • Voting on the itinerary. V3, and the more interesting half of “planning as a group”: eighteen people with opinions about Saturday afternoon is a different screen from eighteen people with a checklist.
  • What a participant's trial actually opens. The doc says time-limited access to the wider product; what that means for their own Places and Plan is unwritten.

8 · The one-paragraph version

Hang a group off a trip: one organiser, an expected count, an invite link, and a checklist of the things the trip already contains — the hotel it is based at, the stops that need booking, and one amount the organiser is out of pocket for. Give the organiser a screen that is the work rather than the roster: four blocks saying what is outstanding, four buttons that chase it, and the twenty-four names underneath. Let a participant open the link, read what is being asked of them, and give a name at the first thing they tap. Take bookings as their word until a partner can confirm them, and leave the money until Stripe Connect and verification exist — everything else works without it. Then add the two things a spreadsheet cannot do: catch the room booked on the wrong night, and put three allergies in front of the kitchen for a table of twenty-six.
